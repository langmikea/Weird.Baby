# THE GIVING RESEARCH

**Date:** 2026-08-05 · **Round:** research only · **Status:** material for Mike's call,
nothing decided, nothing recommended as settled.

**The question, as given:** *"How much to solve immediate vs long term. Help a person
today, invest in change to lean just that much more towards an equal existence."*

No causes have been named by Mike and none are assumed here. The research asks what
already exists in each area and whether these are optimal areas to invest in.

## 1. THE SHORT VERSION

- **The strongest evidence in all of philanthropy is for giving people money.** Not for
  any clever program — for cash. This is true globally and it is true domestically, and
  it is the one finding that survives every methodological fight of the last fifteen
  years.
- **The evidence quality between "immediate relief" and "structural change" is
  radically asymmetric, and that asymmetry is not the same thing as an impact
  difference.** Relief can be measured by randomised trial. Advocacy usually cannot be
  measured at all, because you cannot run the counterfactual on a law. Open
  Philanthropy — the best-resourced organisation ever to try — concluded that the
  largest claimed policy wins are the *hardest* ones to verify. That is a statement
  about measurement, not about value. Choosing relief because it measures well is a
  defensible choice; believing it is better *because* it measures well is a mistake.
- **The best-documented cases where a small amount of advocacy money moved a lot are
  real, and they are also self-reported.** Lead-paint regulation advocacy claims ~$11–14
  per DALY averted, against GiveWell's ~$3,000–5,500 per *life* saved for direct
  delivery. If those numbers held, advocacy would win by orders of magnitude. They are
  preliminary estimates published by the advocates themselves; no independent evaluator
  has confirmed them.
- **The dichotomy in the question is partly false, and specifically it is false in the
  direction that helps.** The Chicago emergency-assistance result — one phone call, one
  cheque, 76% less likely to enter a shelter within six months — is *both* helping a
  person today *and* a structural intervention, because the cascade it prevents
  (shelter, ER, jail) is the mechanism by which one bad month becomes a decade. The
  Kenya cash study found the money did not stop at the recipient: the local multiplier
  was 2.4. Relief at sufficient density becomes a change in the system.
- **It is also partly not false, and pretending otherwise is the sentimental error.**
  Money is finite. The two do compete at the margin. Open Philanthropy spent ~$200m over
  eight years on US criminal justice reform and then concluded global health and
  development was more cost-effective and spun the programme out. That is what a
  well-run structural bet looks like when it does not clearly pay.
- **At one person's scale, the biggest available lever is not *which* cause — it is
  *how* the money arrives.** Unrestricted, multi-year, and unglamorous is where a small
  donor has an advantage that a large institutional funder structurally does not.
  Roughly 12% of institutional grant dollars are both multi-year and unrestricted. A
  private individual can make 100% of theirs that, at no cost, with no board.
- **Half of American households now give nothing.** The share fell from 66% in 2000 to
  49.6% in 2018 and kept falling. Total giving is at record highs because it is
  concentrated in fewer, richer hands. Whatever else is true, the marginal
  small-and-recurring donor is a scarcer thing than they were, and small nonprofits feel
  it directly.

## 2. WHERE MARGINAL DOLLARS DO THE MOST GOOD — THE EVIDENCE AND ITS LIMITS

### 2.1 What the effective-giving literature actually claims

- **GiveWell's headline: roughly $3,000–$5,500 to save a life** through its top-charity
  grants, with a specific 2024 Nigeria malaria grant modelled at about $3,000.
  ([GiveWell](https://www.givewell.org/how-much-does-it-cost-to-save-a-life))
  - **That number is not what most people think it is, and GiveWell says so.** The cost
    of *delivering* a malaria treatment is about $7. The $3,000 figure already absorbs
    downstream effects, indirectly averted deaths, and — importantly — an adjustment for
    how *other* funders would have behaved if you had not given. It is a marginal
    figure, not a unit price.
  - GiveWell expects the cost per life saved to **rise** over time, because the cheapest
    opportunities get funded first.
  - Same programme, different country, wildly different cost-effectiveness. The figure
    is not portable.
- **Scale of the claim:** 2009–2024, more than **$1.45bn** directed to current top
  charities, estimated at roughly **340,000 lives**.
  ([GiveWell](https://www.givewell.org/))
- **The four criteria** are evidence of effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, *room for
  more funding*, and transparency.
  ([criteria](https://www.givewell.org/how-we-work/criteria) ·
  [room for more funding](https://www.givewell.org/how-we-work/criteria/room-for-more-funding))
  - **"Room for more funding" is the one non-obvious criterion and it matters most to a
    small donor.** It asks whether an organisation could actually *use* another dollar.
    A charity with a great programme and no room is a bad marginal gift regardless of
    how good the programme is.

### 2.2 Where the effective-giving apparatus has demonstrably failed or overreached

This section exists because the movement's own record contains the strongest available
counter-evidence to its confidence.

- **The Worm Wars (2015) — the field's own replication crisis.**
  - A 3ie-funded replication by Aiken and Davey of the influential Miguel–Kremer Kenya
    deworming study found a **coding error** in the estimated spillover effects on
    nearby schools: only the nearest 12 schools' populations had been counted. Correcting
    it substantially changed the estimated effects on both infection prevalence and
    school attendance.
  - The day after, **Cochrane updated its review** and found "quite substantial evidence
    that deworming programmes do not show benefit."
    ([CGD's map of the fight](https://www.cgdev.org/blog/mapping-worm-wars-what-public-should-take-away-scientific-debate-about-mass-deworming) ·
    [GiveWell's response](https://blog.givewell.org/2015/07/24/new-deworming-reanalyses-and-cochrane-review/) ·
    [Miguel's side](https://emiguel.econ.berkeley.edu/press/worm-wars))
  - **GiveWell eventually dropped four deworming programmes** from its top charities.
    ([Devex](https://www.devex.com/news/givewell-drops-four-deworming-programs-are-the-worm-wars-back-104076))
  - *The lesson is not "deworming is bad."* It is that a single influential study, run
    by serious people and endorsed by the most rigorous evaluator in the sector, drove
    tens of millions of dollars for over a decade on the strength of a spreadsheet
    nobody had re-run.
- **GiveWell's own mistakes page is the most useful document it publishes.**
  ([Our Mistakes, Dec 2024](https://www.givewell.org/about/our-mistakes/december-2024-version))
  - Bed-net cost-effectiveness rested on an assumption — that only 5% of a population
    would obtain nets outside mass campaigns — **drawn from studies now 30 years old.**
    Correcting it could have lowered the DRC estimate by 15–30%.
  - Their own stated failure mode: parameters "plugged in at face value without
    subjecting the numbers to common-sense scrutiny."
- **The institutional critique — that this whole apparatus systematically cannot see
  structural change.**
  - Berkey, *The Institutional Critique of Effective Altruism* (Utilitas):
    the movement neglects institutional change and directs individuals toward
    organisations that treat symptoms.
    ([PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/The-Institutional-Critique-of-Effective-Altruism.pdf))
  - Amia Srinivasan, *Stop the Robot Apocalypse* (LRB, 2015) — the review that made the
    argument public: the framework systematically underweights political change because
    political change does not produce a number.
    ([LRB](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v37/n18/amia-srinivasan/stop-the-robot-apocalypse))
  - Alice Crary, *Against 'Effective Altruism'* (Radical Philosophy, 2021) — the harder
    version: the metric is not neutral, and choosing to measure this way is itself a
    political act.
    ([Radical Philosophy](https://www.radicalphilosophy.com/article/against-effective-altruism))
  - **The concession the movement itself makes:** it has no clear public standard for
    evaluating advocacy work, and has been criticised for not being upfront that its
    evaluations are therefore *bounded* — they rank what is rankable.
    ([Cambridge/Utilitas](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/utilitas/article/abs/beyond-moral-efficiency-effective-altruism-and-theorizing-about-effectiveness/5C0C26A9FA2312E6471DB136BC2D4130))
- **FTX (2022) and what it did and didn't prove.**
  - The FTX Foundation was a major funder; its collapse removed the money and damaged the
    movement's standing.
    ([Devex](https://www.devex.com/news/what-will-ftx-s-collapse-mean-for-global-health-and-development-104480))
  - The durable critique is not "a donor was a fraud." It is that the movement's wealthiest
    funding has continued shifting toward **long-horizon causes** (AI risk, pandemic
    preparedness) and away from present suffering — Open Philanthropy alone has directed
    $330m+ to AI-risk-adjacent work — while the marketing that recruits ordinary donors
    still runs on bednets.
    ([Worth](https://worth.com/does-effective-altruism-still-work/) ·
    [Current Affairs, hostile](https://www.currentaffairs.org/news/2023/05/why-effective-altruism-and-longtermism-are-toxic-ideologies))
- **The small-donation red team.** An EA Forum post argues small donations are worth
  materially less than headline figures imply, because (a) restricted funds are fungible
  with unrestricted ones, and (b) if a large funder already backs the charity, your
  counterfactual impact is really that funder's *next-best* use of the dollar you freed.
  The counterargument offered is a Shapley-value framing — small donors collectively
  enable the large gift and share its impact.
  ([post](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/oHcRQsiDM9D76aENu/a-red-team-against-the-impact-of-small-donations))
  - ⚠ **Not read directly — 403.** The above is from a search summary. Verify before use.

## 3. THE IMMEDIATE-RELIEF SIDE — WHAT ACTUALLY REACHES A PERSON TODAY

### 3.1 Cash is the best-evidenced thing in the field

- **Kenya, at scale, in Econometrica.** Egger, Haushofer, Miguel, Niehaus and Walker gave
  one-time transfers of ~$1,000 to over 10,500 households across 653 randomised villages
  — a fiscal shock of more than **15% of local GDP**.
  ([Econometrica 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA17945) ·
  [NBER w26600](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26600))
  - Large consumption and asset gains for recipients — expected.
  - **Large positive spillovers onto households and firms that received nothing** — not
    expected, and the important result.
  - **Local transfer multiplier of 2.4.** Every dollar generated about $2.40 of local
    economic activity.
  - **Minimal price inflation** — killing the standard objection that cash just bids up
    prices.
  - Won the Frisch Medal in 2024. This is not a contested finding.
- **The breadth claim:** 200+ independent studies of cash transfers; cash beat *targeted*
  programmes at their own games in Rwanda — better employment effects than job training,
  better child nutrition than nutrition counselling.
  ([GiveDirectly research index](https://www.givedirectly.org/research-on-cash-transfers))
  - Read the incentive: GiveDirectly is the cash organisation summarising the cash
    literature. The Rwanda comparison is real; the framing is theirs.
- **US guaranteed income — the biggest trial, and the most misreported.** OpenResearch
  gave $45m to 3,000 people in Texas and Illinois over three years, $1,000/month vs a
  $50/month control. Two NBER papers.
  ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/sarahemerson/2024/07/22/openai-founder-sam-altman-gave-thousands-of-people-free-money-heres-what-happened/) ·
  [19th News](https://19thnews.org/2024/07/study-guaranteed-income-program-results/))
  - Recipients spent **+$67/mo on food, +$52 on rent, +$50 on transport**, and gave
    **26% more to other people** — mostly family. The money did not stop at the
    recipient.
  - More housing search and more interest in moving, strongest in year three.
  - **They worked slightly fewer hours and earned slightly less.** This is the finding
    that got weaponised in both directions.
    ([Fortune on the misreading](https://fortune.com/2024/09/04/sam-altman-backed-study-guaranteed-income-widely-misinterpreted-ubi-personal-finance))
  - A methodological critique from Guy Standing argues it was not a basic income
    experiment at all.
    ([critique PDF](https://basicincome.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Critique-of-Open-Research-Lab-pilot.pdf))

### 3.2 The single best "help someone today" result in the US literature

- **Chicago Homelessness Prevention Call Center — Evans & Sullivan, *Science*, 2016.**
  ([Notre Dame](https://news.nd.edu/news/emergency-financial-aid-from-call-centers-effectively-prevents-homelessness/))
  - Natural experiment: whether the fund had money on the day you called was effectively
    random. 4,500 callers, 2010–2012.
  - **Callers who reached the centre on a funded day were 76% less likely to enter a
    homeless shelter within six months.**
  - Estimated **~$10,300 per instance of homelessness averted** — and the authors note
    this falls substantially with better targeting toward lower-income callers.
  - Later work by the same lab found emergency financial assistance also **reduces
    violent crime** and changes healthcare use.
    ([Phillips & Sullivan, J. Public Economics 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9004658/))
  - **Why this matters to the question as asked:** it is simultaneously the most
    immediate thing on this list — a person, a phone call, a cheque, today — and a
    structural intervention, because it interrupts the cascade that turns one bad month
    into a decade of instability.

### 3.3 What else has real evidence on the relief side

- **Housing Choice Vouchers** reliably keep low-income families housed and out of
  homelessness. Well-evidenced.
- **Permanent supportive housing** works for people with severe mental illness and for
  veterans. For everyone else the rigorous evidence is thin.
- **Tenant legal representation in eviction court** is *promising* — it improves
  court-related outcomes — but the evidence does not yet say which tactics or programme
  designs are the ones doing the work.
- **Emergency financial assistance + counselling + legal support** prevents homelessness;
  the open question in the literature is **targeting**, not efficacy.
  - All four: [J-PAL, *Reducing and Preventing Homelessness*](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/publication/reducing-and-preventing-homelessness-lessons-randomized-evaluations)
- **Mutual aid networks** did visible, real work during COVID — the Chicago food-security
  study is the best documented — but the causal evidence base is thin, and the model's
  virtues (fast, unbureaucratic, trust-based) are exactly the ones that resist
  measurement.
  ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8458839/))

### 3.4 The honesty question — are overhead claims true?

- **The overhead ratio is a bad metric and the sector's own referees said so in 2013.**
  GuideStar, BBB Wise Giving Alliance and Charity Navigator jointly published an open
  letter to America's donors telling them to stop using it.
  ([the letter](https://d3f9k0n15ckvhe.cloudfront.net/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/OverheadMyth-Letter.pdf) ·
  [NPQ](https://nonprofitquarterly.org/the-overhead-myth/))
- **The "starvation cycle"** — identified by Indiana and Stanford researchers — is the
  mechanism: donor pressure on overhead forces underinvestment in accounting, technology,
  training and evaluation, which makes the organisation worse *and* more fragile, which
  makes it look worse still.
- **Charity Navigator now weights "Impact & Results" at 50% of its score** and has
  de-emphasised overhead accordingly.
  ([methodology guide](https://www.charitynavigator.org/content/dam/cn/cn/landing-pages/Rating%20Methodology%20Guide%20(Updated%20March%202026).pdf))
- **But the harder question is whether the numbers charities publish are honest, and
  there the answer is documented and ugly.** The gift-in-kind valuation scandal:
  ([Forbes / William P. Barrett series](https://www.forbes.com/sites/williampbarrett/2011/11/30/donated-pills-makes-some-charities-look-too-good-on-paper/))
  - **Deworming pills purchasable on world markets for 2¢ were carried on charity
    financial statements at up to $16.25 each — 81,000% above market.**
  - The IRS found Food for the Hungry booked **$93,000 of goods at $76 million**, and
    stated the overstatement was "to mislead the public in order to raise more funds."
    ([IRS audit story](https://www.forbes.com/sites/williampbarrett/2012/01/30/irs-audit-big-charity-filed-misleading-tax-return/))
  - When regulators finally leaned on it, Feed The Children's reported gift-in-kind
    receipts dropped by **$668 million**; MAP International's fell 33%.
  - **The mechanism to understand: inflating non-cash donations inflates the
    denominator, so the overhead *percentage* falls without a single real dollar
    changing hands.** A charity advertising "94% goes to programmes" may be describing
    arithmetic, not conduct.
- **Practical consequence:** a low overhead ratio is not evidence of anything. It is
  either a metric that doesn't measure effectiveness, or a metric that has been gamed.

### 3.5 The relief mechanism that does *not* survive scrutiny

- **Medical crowdfunding is regressive.** A study matching census data to **437,000+
  GoFundMe campaigns, 2016–2020** (AJPH, Feb 2022):
  ([Science News summary](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/medical-gofundme-rarely-helps-most-needy))
  - More campaigns are *started* in low-income, under-insured communities.
  - Campaigns in **affluent, well-insured** communities raise substantially more money.
  - Net effect: the mechanism transfers most to those who need it least, and it
    **deepens** health-care inequity rather than patching it.
  - A separate study of 104,809 cancer campaigns found racial disparities in donation
    amount, donation count, and success.
    ([JMIR 2023](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e51089))
- This is worth sitting with, because sharing a GoFundMe is the most common form of
  "help a person today" there is, and at population level it is running backwards.

## 4. THE LONG-TERM / STRUCTURAL SIDE — WHAT THE EVIDENCE ACTUALLY SUPPORTS

Follow the evidence, not a template. What follows is ordered by strength of evidence,
not by importance.

### 4.1 Strong evidence, long payback

- **Early childhood, birth-to-five, comprehensive.** Heckman's analysis of the
  Abecedarian/CARE programmes finds a **13% return per child per annum** — education,
  health, economic and social outcomes combined, including the value of childcare
  enabling mothers to work. The older Perry Preschool figure is 7–10%.
  ([Heckman Equation](https://heckmanequation.org/) ·
  [UChicago](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/investment-early-childhood-programs-yields-robust-returns))
  - **The honest caveat, and it is a live fight:** these are small, decades-old,
    intensively-run programmes in specific places. Generalising from Perry to national
    policy is contested.
    ([the counter-argument](https://www.ccfp.org/ccfp/how-a-faulty-generalization-is-sabotaging-early-childhood-policy))
- **High-dosage tutoring.** Average effect ~**+0.37 SD** — a median student moves to
  roughly the 66th percentile. Costs $2,500–$4,000/student/year for daily 1:1 or 1:2.
  ([Accelerate research report](https://accelerate.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Accelerate-Research-Report-Efficiency-and-Cost-Effectiveness-1.pdf) ·
  [NBER w32510](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32510/w32510.pdf))
  - **The scale-decay finding is the important one, and it generalises far beyond
    tutoring:** effects fall from **0.44 SD** (<100 students) to **0.30** (100–399) to
    **0.21** (400–999) to **0.16** (1,000+). The thing that works in the pilot is not the
    thing that works at scale.
  - Read this as a general warning about every "proven intervention" pitch: ask at what
    N it was proven.
- **Economic connectedness.** Chetty and Opportunity Insights find that **cross-class
  friendship** is among the strongest predictors of upward mobility for low-income
  children — stronger than school quality or inequality measures.
  ([Opportunity Insights](https://opportunityinsights.org/) ·
  [publications](https://rajchetty.com/publications/))
  - The 2025 *Changing Opportunity* work: **racial gaps are narrowing while class gaps
    widen.** Low-income Black children are doing better; low-income white children are
    flat or worse.
  - **What this does not tell you is what to fund.** "Fund cross-class friendship" is not
    an intervention. The finding is diagnostic, not prescriptive, and that gap is
    exactly where a lot of well-meaning money currently disappears.

### 4.2 Strong evidence, contested politics

- **Pretrial detention and cash bail.** The research base is substantial: pretrial
  detention drives higher conviction rates and longer sentences, plus collateral damage
  to employment, housing and family. Black defendants receive cash bail more often and at
  higher amounts than similarly situated white defendants.
  ([CAP](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/the-case-for-cash-bail-reform/))
  - **Harris County, Texas** misdemeanour bail reform: 6% decrease in new prosecutions
    over three years, 13% increase in releases within 24 hours, 15% drop in guilty pleas,
    17% reduction in likelihood of a jail sentence.
  - **The Bail Project**: 40,000+ people supported; of 35,000+ released on posted bail,
    **92% returned to court.** ([Bail Project](https://bailproject.org/))
  - **Why bail funds are structurally unusual:** the money is *revolving*. A bail
    payment returns to the fund when the case resolves and gets used again. A donation
    here is closer to capital than to expenditure — one of the few places where a small
    donor's dollar genuinely recycles.
- **Voter turnout — the most precisely priced intervention in this entire document.**
  Green and Gerber's *Get Out the Vote*, now incorporating 100+ studies:
  ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/books/get-out-the-vote-2/) ·
  [Gerber & Green overview PDF](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Gerber%20Green%20Handbook.pdf))
  - **Door-to-door canvassing: 1 extra vote per 14 people contacted, ≈$19 per vote.**
  - Leaflets: 1 per 200 contacted, ~$43/vote. Volunteer/paid phone: $35–$200/vote.
    Direct mail: $59–$200/vote.
  - ([Chronicle of Philanthropy on the $19 figure](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/cost-of-door-to-door-canvassing-is-19-per-vote-research-finds/))
  - **The evidence tells you the price of a vote. It cannot tell you which vote.** This
    is the clearest case in the document where the research ends and values begin.

### 4.3 The high-leverage claims — real, cheap, and self-reported

This is where the biggest numbers in philanthropy live, and where the verification is
weakest.

- **Lead Exposure Elimination Project (LEEP).** Advocacy to get lead out of paint in
  low-income countries.
  ([LEEP](https://leadelimination.org/) ·
  [Giving What We Can](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/charities/leep))
  - LEEP's preliminary CEA of its Malawi paint programme: **~$14 per DALY-equivalent
    averted**; a separate estimate gives **~$11, 90% CI [$3.14–$28]**.
  - **Founders Pledge estimate: ~$1.66 to prevent one child's lead exposure**, in
    expectation.
  - A preliminary analysis of lead-adulterated turmeric in Bangladesh came out near
    **$1/DALY**.
    ([EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/aFYduhr9pztFCWFpz/preliminary-analysis-of-intervention-to-reduce-lead-exposure))
  - ⚠ **These are the advocates' own preliminary numbers.** No independent evaluator has
    replicated them. Compare against GiveWell's ~$3,000–5,500 per *life* and the
    magnitude of the claimed gap should itself make you cautious.
- **Tobacco taxation advocacy.** Bloomberg Philanthropies has spent **$1.6bn since 2007**
  and claims **~35 million lives saved** with roughly $1bn of it.
  ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.org/public-health/reducing-tobacco-use/))
  - Independent per-country economic evaluations are more sober and still very good:
    **$74/DALY** for taxation in Mongolia.
    ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10475601/))
  - An EA-side model of *accelerating* a Nepali tax increase by ~six months put expected
    value at **$59/DALY at a 27.5% probability of success** — note the honesty of
    pricing the failure case in.
    ([EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/RRm8vnmwjWK24ung2/taxing-tobacco-the-intervention-that-got-away-happy-world-no))
  - **The counter-evidence, which belongs here:** a critique argues Bloomberg's network
    now dominates the FCTC's evidence pipeline, civil-society participation and policy
    advice — i.e. that at sufficient scale, a philanthropic funder can capture the very
    body of evidence used to evaluate it.
    ([Bate](https://rogerbate.substack.com/p/mapping-philanthropic-capture-in))
- **Corporate campaigning.** The Open Wing Alliance moved **3,000+ food companies** to
  cage-free eggs, affecting 250m+ hens. Whatever one thinks of the cause, it is a
  documented case of small money changing a large system without touching legislation.

### 4.4 What the best-funded attempt to evaluate structural giving concluded

- **Open Philanthropy's own review of policy-oriented philanthropy:** many claims of
  impact exist; **none appear easy to assess; and the most prominent large-scale claimed
  impacts are generally the hardest to evaluate.** Advocacy is underfunded precisely
  because its benefits are diffuse and hard to monetise.
  ([now at Coefficient Giving](https://coefficientgiving.org/research/the-track-record-of-policy-oriented-philanthropy/))
  - ⚠ **Not read directly — 403.** Reported from a search summary. Read before relying.
- **The eight-year bet they placed and then folded.** Open Philanthropy put roughly
  **$200m into US criminal justice reform, 2013–2021** — hundreds of grants — and then
  spun the programme out as an independent organisation, **Just Impact**, with $50m in
  seed funding. Stated rationale: global health and development interventions had
  significantly better cost-effectiveness.
  ([Open Phil](https://www.openphilanthropy.org/focus/criminal-justice-reform/) ·
  [Inside Philanthropy](https://www.insidephilanthropy.com/home/2021-11-16-open-philanthropy-is-spinning-its-justice-reform-program-into-a-major-new-fund-these-are-its-priorities))
  - A detailed independent critique of the bet, worth reading if structural giving is on
    the table: [Nuño Sempere's review](https://nunosempere.com/blog/2022/06/16/criminal-justice/)
  - **Read this two ways and both are legitimate.** Either: a serious funder tried
    structural change, measured honestly, and found direct relief better. Or: they
    applied a measurement framework built for bednets to a domain it cannot see, and
    concluded the domain was worse when what they had actually established was that it
    was harder to score.

## 5. THE SPLIT QUESTION ITSELF

### 5.1 The honest case that the dichotomy is false

- **Relief at density becomes structure.** The Kenya multiplier of 2.4 and the spillovers
  onto non-recipients mean sufficiently dense cash transfer is not "help for a person" —
  it is local economic policy executed privately.
- **Prevention is relief and relief is prevention.** The Chicago result is one cheque
  producing a 76% reduction in shelter entry. The distinction between "help today" and
  "change the trajectory" does not survive contact with that finding.
- **Direct-service organisations are the constituency and the evidence base that
  advocacy runs on.** The people who can testify credibly about eviction are the people
  who staffed the eviction clinic. Defunding relief to fund advocacy can defund the
  advocacy's own credibility.
- **The same organisation can legally do both, and most donors don't know it.** A
  501(c)(3) *may* lobby. Under the **501(h) election** it can spend up to **20% of the
  first $500,000** of exempt-purpose expenditures on lobbying, on a declining scale, to
  a $1m cap — with grassroots lobbying capped at a quarter of that.
  ([National Council of Nonprofits](https://www.councilofnonprofits.org/everyday-advocacy/taking-501h-election))
  - **Practical upshot: a tax-deductible gift to a direct-service charity that has taken
    the 501(h) election funds both sides of Mike's question at once.** Whether a given
    charity has made the election is checkable and rarely advertised.
- **Time asymmetry runs one way.** Structural change may take twenty years. A person in
  crisis has weeks. Relief is partly what makes anyone available for a future at all.

### 5.2 The honest case that it is not false

- **Evaluability is radically asymmetric, and asymmetric evaluability quietly becomes
  asymmetric funding.** Relief has RCTs. Advocacy has counterfactual attribution problems
  nobody has solved. Over time, money flows toward what can be scored — and that is a
  bias, not a judgement.
- **Money is finite and the two do compete at the margin.** Every framework that says
  "both!" without a ratio has answered nothing.
- **Structural bets have variance that relief does not.** $200m and eight years bought
  Open Philanthropy an inconclusive answer. That is a normal outcome for a structural
  bet, not an aberration.
- **Relief is reliable but does not compound. Structural change compounds but may not
  happen.** That is the actual trade, stated without decoration.

### 5.3 What is genuinely known about how to split

- **Nothing gives an optimal ratio.** There is no study of "what percentage should go to
  relief." Anyone offering one is offering a preference.
- **The closest thing to evidence-informed guidance is portfolio logic**, borrowed from
  Open Philanthropy's practice: *worldview diversification* (fund under multiple
  incompatible assumptions about what matters, because you cannot resolve which is right)
  and *hits-based giving* (accept that most structural bets fail, and fund enough of them
  that one landing pays for the rest).
  - **The small-donor translation of hits-based giving is that it doesn't translate.**
    Hits-based logic requires a portfolio large enough for one hit to redeem many
    failures. One person giving to one or two structural bets is not running a
    portfolio; they are placing a bet. That is allowed — but it should be named as a bet,
    not as a strategy.
- **The one structural asymmetry that does favour splitting:** relief has a floor (the
  money reaches someone, and that is real even if nothing else happens) while structural
  giving has a real chance of producing literally nothing. A split is a way of buying a
  floor.

## 6. SMALL-DONOR REALITY — WHAT CHANGES AT ONE PERSON'S SCALE

### 6.1 The context Mike is giving into

- **Half of American households have stopped giving.** From **66% in 2000 to 49.6% in
  2018**, per the Philanthropy Panel Study (Lilly Family School of Philanthropy, 9,000+
  households tracked).
  ([IU News](https://news.iu.edu/live/news/38127-philanthropy-report-explores-pandemics-impact-on) ·
  [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/charity-donations-us-households-decline/))
- **Nearly 8 in 10 households with >$200k wealth give; fewer than 4 in 10 below $50k
  do.** Total giving is at records because it has concentrated upward.
- **What this means practically:** small organisations have lost their base of small
  recurring donors and become dependent on a few large ones. A reliable individual donor
  is a scarcer and more useful thing than the sector's headline totals suggest.

### 6.2 The advantages a small donor actually has

These are real and they are structural, not consolation.

- **Unrestricted money.** Institutional funders overwhelmingly restrict. Only **21%** of
  grants pre-COVID were general operating support; only **12.4%** were *both* multi-year
  and unrestricted (CEP).
  ([CEP](https://cep.org/blog/strengthening-nonprofits-the-value-of-complementing-multiyear-gos-grants-with-capacity-building-supports/))
  - **93% of nonprofit leaders say general operating support moderately or significantly
    strengthened their ability to achieve their mission.**
  - The MacKenzie Scott natural experiment — **$19bn to 2,000+ organisations in five
    years, averaging ~$5m against a CEP study median of $123k** — is the largest test of
    unrestricted giving ever run. CEP's three-year study (800+ nonprofit leaders, 243
    foundation leaders) finds strengthened financial stability, reserves, talent
    investment, and increased community impact.
    ([CEP, *Breaking the Mold*, 2025](https://cep.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/CEP_Breaking_the_Mold_FNL.pdf))
  - **CEP's own caveat, stated plainly: it is too early to draw long-term conclusions;
    it could take decades.**
  - **The transferable point:** an individual writes unrestricted cheques by default, at
    no cost, with no board to justify it to. This is the single largest quality advantage
    a private donor has over a foundation, and it costs nothing to exercise.
- **Speed.** No grant cycle, no RFP, no reporting requirement. A private donor can fund
  something in an afternoon that a foundation would take nine months to approve.
- **Risk tolerance and willingness to fund unglamorous things.** No reputational exposure,
  no need for a photogenic outcome, no obligation to explain a failure. The rent on the
  office. The case-management software. The salary of the person who does the intake
  calls. These are the things everyone underfunds and they are the things a small donor
  can fund without asking anyone.
- **Relationship.** A donor who knows one organisation over years knows things about it
  that no 990 contains, and can direct money on that knowledge. This is not measurable
  and it is not nothing.

### 6.3 The mechanics, honestly assessed

- **Recurring vs lump.**
  - `[INDUSTRY FIGURE]` Vendor-published statistics claim recurring-donor retention of
    ~83% vs ~45% for one-time, and lifetime values 4x higher. These come from fundraising
    software companies with a direct interest in selling recurring-giving modules. Treat
    the direction as plausible and the magnitudes as marketing.
  - **The defensible reason for recurring giving does not depend on those numbers:**
    predictable income is what allows an organisation to hire, plan, and stop
    re-fundraising its entire budget annually. That is a mechanism, not a statistic.
  - **The defensible reason for lump giving:** it clears the new 0.5% AGI floor (§6.4)
    and it is the form in which unrestricted, unexpected money does the most good.
  - These are not exclusive. Recurring base + occasional lump is the shape most of the
    reasoning supports.
- **Matching gifts — real, but the ratio is theatre.** Karlan & List's field experiment
  with 50,000+ prior donors:
  ([NBER w12338](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12338/w12338.pdf))
  - Mentioning a match increased **revenue per solicitation by 19%** and the probability
    of donating by **22%**.
  - **Larger match ratios (2:1, 3:1) produced no additional effect over 1:1.** "Triple
    your impact!" is worth exactly what "double your impact!" is worth.
  - Effects varied sharply by geography — the match is not a universal lever.
  - **Implication for Mike as a giver rather than a fundraiser:** a match is a reason to
    time a gift, not a reason to choose a charity. And the honest question about any
    match is whether the matching money would have arrived anyway — usually it would.
- **Giving circles.** 4,000+ in the US, 370,000 participants, **$3.1bn given 2017–2023**,
  more than doubling in seven years.
  ([Johnson Center / Philanthropy Together](https://johnsoncenter.org/us-collective-giving-research-initiative/))
  - Collective giving groups are far more likely than institutional foundations to fund
    human rights, racial equity, gender equity. 60% of 2023 collective giving came from
    women-only circles.
  - **The evidence is overwhelmingly about effects on the donors, not the recipients:**
    91% report improved sense of belonging, 77% feel their voice matters, 77% report
    learning effects. Almost nothing measures whether circle-directed money outperforms
    the same money given alone.
  - **Honest read: a giving circle is well-evidenced as a way to give more, more
    consistently, and with more knowledge. It is not evidenced as a way to give better.**
- **Donor-advised funds.** ([NPT / IPS / Baker Institute coverage](https://www.bakerinstitute.org/research/do-donor-advised-funds-need-more-regulation))
  - **What they're for:** contribute now (take the deduction now), grant out later;
    donate appreciated stock without paying capital gains; anonymity if wanted.
  - **The payout numbers are contested and the gap is instructive.** Sponsor-reported
    aggregate payout ~25.3%; the 2025 Institute for Policy Studies independent report
    puts the **median** at ~9–10%. Community foundations ~17.1%, national sponsors ~25.4%
    over three years. There is **no legal minimum payout** for a DAF at all.
    - Aggregate vs median is the whole fight: a few very active accounts can carry a
      sector average while the typical account sits.
  - **The "DAFs democratise giving" claim is weak.** Average account size fell from
    $279,479 (2017) to $182,842 — which the industry cites as evidence of small-donor
    adoption. Someone with $180,000 to give away is not a small donor.
    ([Inequality.org](https://inequality.org/article/donor-advised-fund-numbers/))
  - **For a donor at Mike's scale the case for a DAF is narrow but real:** it is
    genuinely useful for donating appreciated stock, and for bunching gifts into one tax
    year while paying them out over several. It is not useful as a place to park money
    you intend to give this year anyway.

### 6.4 US tax mechanics as of 2026 — changed this year, and it matters

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act changed the calculus for exactly this size of donor,
effective **tax year 2026**.

- **If you take the standard deduction:** a **new above-the-line deduction of $1,000
  (single) / $2,000 (married filing jointly)** for **cash** gifts to 501(c)(3)s. It
  reduces AGI even without itemising, and it is **not** subject to the new floor below.
  - **This is the first time since 2021 that a non-itemiser gets anything at all for
    giving.**
- **If you itemise:** a new **0.5% of AGI floor**. Only contributions *above* 0.5% of
  AGI are deductible. On $300,000 AGI, the first $1,500 of giving deducts nothing.
  - **Direct consequence: bunching now pays.** Two years of giving in one year clears the
    floor once instead of twice. This is the mechanical argument for lump over recurring,
    and it is the main legitimate use of a DAF at this scale.
  - Sources: [Fidelity Charitable](https://www.fidelitycharitable.org/articles/obbb-tax-reform.html) ·
    [TurboTax summary](https://turbotax.intuit.com/tax-tips/tax-deductions-and-credits/one-big-beautiful-bill-charitable-deduction-changes-for-2026/c10OQHJpK)
  - ⚠ Tax specifics should be confirmed against current IRS guidance or an accountant
    before acting. This document is research, not tax advice.
- **501(c)(3) vs 501(c)(4) — the fork that matters for structural giving.**
  - **501(c)(3):** tax-deductible; lobbying limited (substantial-part test by default, or
    the 501(h) expenditure test); **no** electoral campaign activity at all.
  - **501(c)(4):** **unlimited** lobbying; **not** tax-deductible.
  - **The trade is explicit: the more directly political the giving, the less the tax
    code subsidises it.** Many advocacy operations run a paired c3/c4. Which entity you
    give to is a real decision, not an administrative detail.
    ([GRF](https://www.grfcpa.com/resource/501c4-vs-501c3-which-do-i-choose/) ·
    [Tenenbaum](https://www.tenenbaumlegal.com/articles/limitations-on-lobbying-activities-everything-that-501c3-organizations-need-to-know/))

### 6.5 Vetting — what to actually check, and why the raters disagree

- **[IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/tax-exempt-organization-search)** — the only definitive answer to
  "is this organisation currently tax-exempt." Check this first, always.
- **[ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/)** — Form 990s back to 2001, 1.8m+ filings.
  Shows revenue, expenses, executive compensation. Free, no account.
  ([ProPublica's own how-to-vet guide](https://www.propublica.org/article/how-to-vet-nonprofits-before-you-give))
- **[CharityWatch](https://www.charitywatch.org/)** — the forensic one. Re-classifies costs that charities book as
  programme expense (notably telemarketing and direct mail with a token educational
  message attached). Small coverage, harsh grades.
- **[Charity Navigator](https://www.charitynavigator.org/)** — broad coverage (9,000+), now weighting Impact & Results at
  50%, with cost-per-outcome methodology built with Impact Genome across 17 programme
  areas so far.
- **[Candid / GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/)** — profiles and self-reported data; breadth, not judgement.
- **The disagreements are the signal, not noise.** The National Cancer Coalition rated as
  a star performer on Charity Navigator while receiving an **F** from CharityWatch — the
  entire difference being how joint telemarketing costs are classified.
  ([Seattle Times](https://www.seattletimes.com/life/lifestyle/watchdogging-the-charity-watchdogs/))
  - **Practical rule: if two raters disagree sharply, read the 990 yourself. The
    disagreement is telling you where the accounting choice is.**

### 6.6 The psychology, named so it can be accounted for

Not a criticism of anyone's instincts — a description of known effects that operate
regardless of intelligence or good faith.

- **Identifiable victim effect.** People give substantially more to one named, pictured
  person than to statistical victims. Replicated, though with debate about magnitude.
  ([Collabra replication](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/9/1/90203/199223/Revisiting-and-Rethinking-the-Identifiable-Victim))
- **Scope insensitivity.** The perceived value of saving one life does **not** multiply
  when the number becomes two, or a thousand. The affective system is driven by images
  and narratives and is close to blind to numbers.
  - **This is the mechanism that makes "help a person today" feel more real than "shift a
    system."** Both are real. Only one of them produces a face.
- **Warm glow is real and significant** — giving genuinely makes people happier, and
  happier people give more. Not a flaw; it is a large part of why giving is sustainable.
  ([Andreoni's original theory](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4892936_Impure_Altruism_and_Donations_to_Public_Goods_A_Theory_of_Warm-Glow_Giving))
- **Moral licensing — evidence mixed, do not over-apply.** The claim that a good deed
  licenses a later bad one is popular; at least one study found the opposite (moral
  *reinforcement* — people gave more after recalling a moral act). Unsettled.

## 8. WHAT REQUIRES MIKE'S VALUES, NOT EVIDENCE

No amount of further research resolves any of these. They are the actual load-bearing
questions and every framework in §1–§7 quietly assumes an answer to them.

- **Distance.** A dollar demonstrably does more measured good far away than near. Whether
  that *should* determine where it goes is not an empirical question. Nobody's research
  can tell you how much a stranger's proximity is worth.
- **Time.** A person helped this month against a system changed in twenty years. There is
  no discount rate that evidence supplies. The one you choose is a value.
- **Risk.** A 95% chance of modest, certain good, or a 5% chance of enormous good. Both
  are defensible. Expected-value maths gives an answer that most people reject on
  reflection, and the rejection is not irrational.
- **"Equal existence" — outcomes or opportunity?** These have entirely separate evidence
  bases and largely non-overlapping interventions. The phrase in the question does not
  decide between them, and choosing decides most of what follows.
- **Relationship or optimisation.** Whether you want to *know* what you fund — the
  people, the place, the failure modes — or to allocate at a distance to whatever scores
  best. Knowing costs efficiency. Optimising costs knowledge. There is no version that
  is both.
- **Contested ground.** For the politically contested interventions in §4.2, the evidence
  prices the tactic and says nothing about the direction. That choice is entirely a
  values choice and should be made as one, openly, rather than smuggled in under a
  cost-per-vote figure.
- **Whether counterfactual reasoning is a duty or a trap.** Taken seriously, "would this
  have happened anyway" can argue almost any individual gift down to near-zero impact. It
  is a rigorous idea that, applied without limit, dissolves the case for giving at all.
  Where to stop applying it is a judgement, not a finding.

## 9. THE DECISIONS HE FACES

One line each. Answerable one at a time, in any order. None has a right answer in the
research.

1. **Near or far** — does the money stay where you live, go where a dollar buys most, or split?
2. **What ratio, if split** — relief vs structural, stated as a number you'd actually hold to?
3. **Few or many** — one or two organisations known deeply, or a spread across several?
4. **How much, and against what** — a percentage of income, a fixed annual figure, or ad hoc?
5. **Recurring, lump, or both** — knowing the 0.5% AGI floor now rewards bunching if you itemise?
6. **Restricted or unrestricted** — will you name what the money is for, or hand it over?
7. **Deductible or effective** — will you give to 501(c)(4)s at all, giving up the deduction for unlimited advocacy?
8. **Anonymous or named** — do you want your name on it, and does being known to an organisation matter to you?
9. **Do you want to know them** — relationship with one or two organisations, or allocation at a distance?
10. **Alone or with others** — solo giving, or a giving circle for the knowledge and the discipline?
11. **What's your failure tolerance** — is a structural bet that produces nothing acceptable, and how many times?
12. **What do you want back** — nothing, a report, a person you actually meet? An honest answer here changes everything above it.
13. **Does it need to be a cause** — or is "wherever there's a person in front of me this month" a legitimate strategy? (The evidence says it is not obviously worse than the alternatives, and §3.5 says the common version of it runs backwards.)
14. **How much of this do you want to revisit** — set it once and leave it, or review annually?
