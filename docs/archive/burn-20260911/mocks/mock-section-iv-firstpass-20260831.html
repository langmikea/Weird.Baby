                                  SECTION IV

                           CONTROLS AND INDICATORS


4-1.    GENERAL.

        This section identifies the controls, the indicators and the inputs of
        the instrument, and says what each is for. They are shown in Figure
        4-1 and are listed in Table 4-1.

        How the instrument is used is the matter of Section VI and how it is
        built is the matter of Section VII. Neither is repeated here. This
        section is concerned with what the operator meets on the outside of
        the instrument and with nothing behind it.

        The instrument asks little of the operator's hands. The dial and the
        shutter carry the whole of the menu system between them and are
        described at paragraph 4-3. What else the instrument reads it reads
        from being handled: from movement, at paragraph 4-5, and from sound,
        at paragraph 4-7.

        The designations under which the inputs are scheduled are given in
        Table 2-2 and are not repeated in this section.

4-3.    THE DIVISION OF CONTROL.

        The dial and the shutter are the controls of the instrument and their
        duties do not overlap. The distinction between them is the one thing
        an operator must hold, and every operation of the menu system follows
        from it.

        The dial moves and does not choose. It carries the operator through
        the rows in the order they stand in, and it does nothing else. Turning
        it commits nothing. A row the dial has reached is a row arrived at and
        not a row taken, and the instrument does not act upon it.

        The shutter chooses. It takes the row the dial has arrived at, and
        taking is the whole of its office. The shutter does not move between
        rows and cannot be made to.

        They are therefore not interchangeable and neither will do the other's
        work. An operator who has turned the dial has not chosen; an operator
        who has used the shutter has taken the row the dial was resting upon.
        The procedure of paragraph B-3 is written in these terms, and every
        operation described in Section VI is worked with these controls and no
        others.

4-4

        The division holds everywhere upon the instrument. There is no surface
        on which the dial takes a row and none on which the shutter moves
        between them.

4-5.    MOTION INPUT.

        The instrument reads its own movement. This is an input and not a
        control: there is nothing to set and nothing to aim, and the
        instrument is simply handled.

        A shake is a request for a determination. The manner of putting a
        question and taking the determination is given at paragraph 6-5, and a
        further determination at paragraph 6-7; neither is repeated here.

        The same movement is read differently according to when it is made. A
        shake made before a question has been put is not read as a request,
        and shakes made early and repeatedly call instead for the
        recalibration of paragraph 8-7. A shake made while a determination is
        still standing does not produce another.

        Stillness is read as well as movement. The maintenance routine of
        paragraph 8-7 requires the instrument to be held still and does not
        proceed while it is handled.

        Movement is read whenever the instrument is in service. It is not read
        while the instrument is off; see paragraph 7-19.

4-7.    ACOUSTIC INPUT.

        The instrument hears. This also is an input and not a control, and it
        requires nothing of the operator.

        Voice control is offered as a novelty and is to be treated as such. It
        is not the means by which the instrument is worked. Every function of
        the instrument is reached with the dial and the shutter, and an
        operator who never speaks to it is prevented from nothing.

        The instrument does not take a spoken word for an instruction, and a
        determination asked for aloud is not asked for. A determination is
        called for by the movement of paragraph 4-5 and by no other means.

        What the instrument makes of what it hears is not described in this
        manual.

        The audible output is a separate system and is not the matter of this
        paragraph. It is given at paragraph 7-17.

4-9.    INDICATOR LAMP.

        The indicator lamp narrates. It states what the instrument is about,
        in a form which can be read across a room and without the glass being
        looked at. It is not a control and there is nothing to set upon it.

        The conditions it shows are given in Table 4-2. The lamp reports and
        it does not warn: a colour shown is a state the instrument is in, and
        no colour upon the lamp is a fault reported. Faults are the matter of
        Section IX.

        At starting the lamp narrates the sequence. It shows red while the
        instrument is coming up and the displays are not yet driven, green
        when they are driven and the sequence is running, and orange when the
        start is complete. The sequences themselves are the matter of Section
        V.

        The lamp is also how the instrument makes a system message known. It
        shows that a message is held; it does not announce an arrival, and
        paragraph 6-23 governs. The message itself is read at the Messages
        door; see paragraph 6-23 and Table 6-2.
