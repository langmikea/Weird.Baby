# THE ELEMENTS — reels assembled from building blocks (50,000 ft, 2026-09-12)

Mike (cleaned): "I am going to take photos and videos of what I will call
elements, or building blocks: a front view, an isometric view, a back view,
the unit held in the hand, a finger on the scroll knob and on the button. The
idea is to remove as much friction as possible from generating a video once a
question and an answer are selected. It might be acceptable to use a simulated
screen; they did this on TV selling TVs all the time. If not, I at least want
to program the VIIIp to flash some generic imagery. Planned well enough, the
blocks should look like a multi-camera shoot. Think broadly: could this help
the music videos too. Best practices, off-the-shelf, what we already built; do
not reinvent the wheel. Do not race ahead and start solving things."

This is a discussion paper, not a plan. Nothing is built from it until ruled.

## 1. The read

It is SED applied to video: a library of fixed shots plus one line of data
per day (the question, the answer, the feature). The reel line is already the
assembly half: normalise, add the question line, add the pop, caption,
schedule. What the line lacks is the pictures in the middle: the machine, and
the answer on its glass. The elements idea fills exactly that hole. Good
direction; the friction it removes is the friction that broke the first plan.

## 2. What we already own

- **The twin**, the machine's full software in the browser. It draws the
  display itself, at the machine's own resolution. It can be put in any
  state, any feature, any answer, in a second, and it is the same software
  the unit runs.
- **The display emulator** (robots tools), which renders the display from the
  content tables with the machine's own font.
- **A composite precedent.** The MGK-VIIIp cover's BIOS beat: the twin's
  framebuffer sampled at a labelled beat and composited into the front-view
  photograph at the measured aperture. The technique exists and was passed.
- **The sounds**: the electrical short, the pop, the machine's own set.
- **The reel line** (ffmpeg): 1080x1920, the pop, captions, schedule, Buffer.
- **The tray setup** for the album plates: the same afternoon shoots the
  elements from the same positions.
- **The catalogue**: the feature per launch day, so the line knows which glass
  state each day needs.

## 3. The glass: three ways, and the one we should not do

- **A. The twin's display, composited into a real plate.** Shoot one static
  close-up plate of each unit's glass; the twin renders the answer; the line
  composites it at the measured aperture with the CRT's own bloom. Honest by
  construction: the display is the machine's own software showing the
  machine's own answer, and forcing an answer is a setting, never a shoot.
  Any feature, any state, on demand. Recommended.
- **B. The real unit in attract mode, the twin for the close-up.** Flash the
  unit with a generic moving pattern so the hand and knob elements have a
  live glass, and use A for the answer shot. Needs the firmware flash, which
  is on the calendar for 10-15 with you at the bench.
- **C. The real unit, every day.** The current plan and the friction you are
  escaping. Keep for the occasional true one-take.
- **Not the AI-simulated screen.** It would be inventing what the machine
  says on the glass, which is the one thing the house never does, and it is
  unnecessary: the twin is the real screen.

## 4. The multi-camera look

Shoot once per unit, one afternoon, the tray setup extended: front, isometric,
back, in hand, finger on the scroll knob, finger on the igniter, the shake,
plus the static glass plate. Each vantage as a photograph and as three to
five seconds of video with a little life in it. Then three or four cut lists,
not one, so no two days are the same cut: wide, insert, knob, glass with the
answer, hold, pop. Fifteen seconds. The industry's own rule for this is the
same: a tagged library of a handful of angles per subject, shot two or three
versions each, cuts the edit from an hour to minutes.

## 5. The engine, off the shelf or ours

- **Ours first**: ffmpeg (in the line already) plus a headless browser to
  render the twin's glass. No vendor, no fee, no new format.
- **Remotion**: video from React, the museum's own stack; the twin's display
  could sit inside a composition directly. Free for individuals. More work,
  more motion.
- **Hosted template APIs** (Creatomate, Shotstack): JSON timelines rendered
  in the cloud. Clean, paid, a vendor between us and the file; nothing here
  needs them yet.
- **Phone template apps** (CapCut, Canva): hand work every day, which is the
  friction we are removing.

## 6. The risk to name

Both platforms now throttle repetitive, templated patterns and reward
original footage. So: the elements are real footage of the real object and
the real hands; several cut lists; one fresh shot a week; clean files, never
a cross-posted watermark. The Determination stays a real question with a
real answer; only the pictures around it are assembled.

## 7. Where else it pays

- **The music videos**: the same idea, one audio master, four or five
  performance angles, cut lists; the produced version's video without a crew.
- **The album plates**: the same shoot day.
- **The wing**: the twin's rendered glass as the live picture on every
  What It Does entry, per feature.
- **The fan-question segment**: batch-rendered from the same elements.
- **The manual**: the display states as illustrations, from the emulator.

## 8. The questions, in order

1. The glass: A the twin composite · B attract mode plus the twin · C the real
   unit every day.
2. The engine: A ours (ffmpeg + headless twin) · B Remotion · C a hosted API.
3. The element list per unit: A the eight above · B add or drop, say which ·
   C a different list.
4. The music videos: A the same method for the produced versions · B live
   only, no assembly · C later.

## 10. The question being put (Mike, 2026-09-12, cleaned)

"It has to be easily readable, designed to be read with ease, frictionless,
because many users will not have the sound on. While the question is being
asked, one of the views should be the screen on, with an oscilloscope-type
view, indicating the machine is hearing spoken words. The audio must not be a
robot: a human, or like the adults in Charlie Brown, an indiscernible series
of sounds that still leaves the impression of spoken words. Not the Software
Automated Mouth; that would confuse whether the machine is asking or
answering. Another layer of elements: sometimes an underlay or an overlay,
the Fourth of July for instance, flags, or the unit on a flag. Examples of
where my mind was, not specific things to do."

Ops' read: the text is already ruled burned in (09-12); it becomes the
largest thing on the frame while the question is put, and stays until the
answer. The "hearing" view is a display state of the machine, so it is the
twin's to draw: a live trace on the top monitor (the diagnostic display that
never stops reading the room) while the front glass waits, or on the front
glass itself. The voice: a human reading daily is friction; a neural voice
is a second machine voice; the Charlie Brown adult is on-brand, says nothing
a sound-off viewer misses, and can be generated per question with a cadence
taken from the syllables so it reads like the words. Overlays are free on any
footage; underlays need a keyable ground, which means one setting shot for
it. Questions: the voice; the trace's screen; the occasion layer.

**Ruled 2026-09-12 (voice): A, the Charlie Brown adult.** The question is
voiced by an indiscernible muted voice, generated per question with a
cadence from the words' syllables; never the machine's voice, never a
recording task for Mike.

**Ruled 2026-09-12 (the trace): the top screen, the fluidic suspension.**
Mike: "It goes on the screen a user would be looking at when speaking. The

**Ruled 2026-09-12 (the occasion layer): C, later.** Overlays and underlays
